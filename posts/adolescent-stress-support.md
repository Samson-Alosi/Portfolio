Adolescence brings change, growing independence, and new responsibilities. Some stress is a normal response to challenges, but ongoing or overwhelming stress can affect a young person's wellbeing. Adults can help by listening with curiosity, offering predictable support, and working with the adolescent rather than trying to solve everything for them.

### Common sources of stress

Stressors differ for each young person. They may include:

- Exams, school workload, attendance, or uncertainty about the future.
- Friendship changes, conflict, bullying, or pressure to fit in.
- Social media, online comparison, or difficult digital interactions.
- Changes at home, family conflict, financial pressure, grief, or caring responsibilities.
- Puberty, identity, body image, growing independence, or feeling misunderstood.
- Lack of sleep, a busy schedule, or not enough time to rest and connect with others.

### Signs to notice

Stress does not look the same in every adolescent. Possible signs include changes from their usual behavior, such as irritability, worry, tearfulness, withdrawal, difficulty concentrating, changes in sleep or appetite, frequent headaches or stomach aches, reduced interest in activities, or a sudden change in school participation. One sign alone does not establish a cause or diagnosis. Notice patterns and ask gently rather than assuming.

### Ways to offer support

1. **Choose a calm moment and listen.** Use open questions such as, “What has been weighing on you lately?” Give them time to answer, and avoid rushing to correct or lecture.
2. **Acknowledge their experience.** You can take their feelings seriously even when the problem seems small from an adult perspective. Try, “That sounds like a lot to manage.”
3. **Ask what kind of help they want.** They may want someone to listen, help making a plan, or support speaking with a teacher or another trusted adult.
4. **Break the problem into manageable steps.** Work together to identify what can be changed now, what needs support, and what can wait.
5. **Protect the basics.** Encourage regular sleep, meals, breaks, enjoyable activities, and movement without turning these into extra demands or punishments.
6. **Keep communication open with consent and care.** When school or another adult needs to be involved, explain why and include the adolescent as much as possible, while prioritizing their safety.

### When to seek extra help

Consider contacting a qualified mental health professional, health provider, or school counselor if distress is persistent, worsening, interfering with daily life, or difficult to manage with family support. If an adolescent talks about suicide or self-harm, take it seriously, stay with them if there may be immediate danger, and seek urgent help from local emergency or mental health services. Do not promise to keep an immediate safety concern secret.

Support does not mean having every answer. A calm, reliable adult who listens, stays connected, and helps a young person access appropriate support can make an important difference.
